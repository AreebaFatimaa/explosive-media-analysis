Meet Akhbar Enfejari, the Iranian channel behind the Lego war videos

What three months of Telegram posts can say about the team

By Areeba Fatima
Scroll to begin ↓

On March 7, 2026, a new kind of war-related content hit social media platforms almost all at once. In a two-minute video, Lego Trump and Lego Netanyahu pore over an animated "Epstein File." Aggravated, Trump slams a red button, launching an airstrike on the Shajareh Tayyebeh girls' elementary school in Minab, Iran. The Lego-style animation cuts through one moment of war after another, each hard to forget: a pink schoolbag belonging to one of the girls killed in the Minab airstrike, which Amnesty International says killed 168 students.

The video shows the British base in Cyprus under attack, airstrikes in Tel Aviv, hits on the U.S. embassy in Saudi Arabia and the U.S. base in Bahrain. It shows the Strait of Hormuz shutting down, and a flag-draped coffin being carried off an aircraft by tiny Lego soldiers, and closes with a message: "In remembrance of the 168 students from Minab who were martyred at the hands of Zionist and American terrorists" — a reference to the Feb. 28 strike.

News organizations around the world rushed to react. In an on-screen analysis, a France 24 anchor said, "What's incredibly interesting here is they [the Iranian government] are presenting to us the vulnerability of their own civilians whilst also in a way showing the United States and Israel as the aggressors."

While several journalists have taken a stab at attempting to articulate who these people really are, only few were able to speak to them, and no one has really analyzed the explosive content they were sharing on their Telegram channel, the primary source of dissemination of their message.

The Explosive Media Telegram channel

Explosive Media — Akhbar Enfejari in Persian — is the team of young Iranians behind the AI-generated Lego videos powering the Iranian government's war narrative on American social media. Banned from YouTube and blocked on Instagram, Facebook and X, the team now has five songs and 43,600 monthly listeners on Spotify, nearly 40,000 followers on Instagram and 79,600 on X — and has spearheaded a trend that has American media commentators declaring the White House lost the propaganda war this round.

Its longest-running accessible account in the U.S. is its Persian-language Telegram channel, set up during the 12-day war last year.

A look at what that channel was sharing in the weeks before the U.S. struck Iran in February opens a window onto Iranian society — and onto how a small group of young people came to lead the country's wartime state media efforts.

They posted weather updates, memes about exam nights, international football scores, film recommendations, foreign news and pointed criticism of the government's handling of the economy, often dozens of times a day.

Only the channel's admin posts. From Dec. 31, 2025, to April 9, 2026 — a window that brackets the 40-day U.S.-Iran war that began Feb. 28 — the channel published 2,840 messages: 981 videos, 1,665 images and 194 text dispatches.

This time frame includes 58 days before the war officially started on Feb. 28, and the 40 days of the war.

I transcribed every post, translated it from Persian, classified it by theme and keyword, and cross-referenced by subject. Videos were sampled into key-frame screenshots. The patterns that emerge are the subject of this piece. How the dataset was built, and how accurate the classifications are →

From anti-regime to pro-regime in one night

In January the channel platformed anti-regime voices: Amin Salimi telling people to protest "within a framework," mockery of Mohammad Reza Farzin's appointment as economic advisor, Foad Izadi warning not to trust Axios.

Anti-regime posts ran through January and most of February — criticism of the economy, of prices, of officials' answers. They never dominated the feed, but they were never absent from it either.

Then at 01:22 AM on February 28, the first US–Israeli strike hit Tehran. The feed became a live battle tracker.

By mid-March it was producing Lego-animated music videos in praise of the IRGC and Ayatollah Khamenei.

After the war began, anti-regime posts all but stopped: four in 255 posts, 1.6% of output, against the 15 the pre-war rate would predict.

The page took a 14-day break from publishing in January, and then another right after the war began. If they had decided to collaborate with the Iranian government, it must have been after the war, after which anti-regime posting all but ceased.

In total, the channel has shared 1,138 posts which were classified as 'pro-regime,' and 156 posts classified as 'anti-regime,' leaving 1,546 which did not fit in either category.

Share of posts taking a regime stance

On February 28, the pro-regime share of the channel's output rose from 38.4% before the war to 57.3% after, while the anti-regime share fell from 5.9% to 1.6%.

Through January and February, there was a clear presence of anti-regime messaging, reaching its highest, almost 10% of all messages in the week after January 4. After the war began, anti-regime posts entirely disappeared and the pro-regime posts were reintroduced after a week-long blackout at their highest ever point, nearing 90%. And the single well-supported post-war week of March 9, with more than 82 posts, shows a 76% jump in pro-regime messages.

When the bricks arrived

While the channel appeared to be experimenting a little with LEGO-themed content, it picked up exponentially after the war. LEGO content barely existed before the war, 5 posts, 0.19% of pre-war output, and then jumps to 19 posts, 7.45% of post-war output, a roughly 39-fold increase in share. Eight of the 24 appeared on 14 March alone.

What the classification surfaced is not licensed LEGO merchandise but the channel's own brick-built productions: minifigure scenes with Persian captions, CGI renders of brick-built objects, figures holding Iranian flags, a crowd of brick-headed figures, minifigures with Molotov cocktails.

The Slopaganda Duel

The White House was already shoulder-deep in the meme world, raiding American internet culture, films and songs in a vain attempt to drum up confidence in the war — at times tipping into outright war-mongering. Officials paired strike footage released by U.S. Central Command with clips from "Iron Man," "Gladiator," "Top Gun" and the video game "Call of Duty," along with rap songs riding TikTok trends and live baseball broadcasts.

Critics swiftly labeled the output "slopaganda" — a term coined by Michał Klincewicz of Tilburg University, Australian philosopher Mark Alfano of Macquarie University and independent media researcher Amir Ebrahimi Fard.

It was lazy, in bad taste, and didn't land with most Americans, who had already turned against the war.

The Trump administration's hypermasculine, frenzied gamification of the conflict drew fire even from former White House officials. Kristopher Purcell, a Bush administration aide, told PBS News, "This gamefication of war is really appalling, especially when you consider the administration's typical response to mass shootings, which is to blame violent video games and movies."

The Lego videos, by contrast, were a runaway hit on social media, racking up millions of views across platforms.

Calling itself the "Lego Resistance Front," the team announced in a Telegram post: "LRF has found new friends," pointing to copycat accounts that had picked up the style and produced their own Lego-style videos targeting Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, Melania Trump and other administration officials.

Writing for 404 Media, Matthew Gault put it bluntly: "Iran is attempting to speak to the broader American public. Trump is confident he only has to impress the online freaks he thinks still love him."

'We love the American people'

The BBC interviewed the team on April 11, using only a snippet in its published piece. While that segment marked the first time anyone from Explosive Media appeared on screen, face obscured in shadow, it revealed little about who they are or what they think.

"Lego is simple, Lego is charming, and Lego is understood by all," a member of the team told me when I asked why they had chosen Lego-inspired AI animation to push the Iranian government's view of the war.

In an interview, the member said the team has fewer than 10 people, all aged 25 or under. He grew up watching "Captain Tsubasa," "Brave Boy" and "Robin Hood." "We use a mix of AI tools, animation software and editing techniques, but I won't name them. That's part of the magic." The team has not been contacted by the Lego Group, he said, and relies on AI tools developed in-house or accessed via VPN to get around sanctions.

YouTube and Instagram have taken down the team's accounts repeatedly, he said. "Our pages have been shut down multiple times. But it's never really stopped us. We have friends all over the world who keep sharing our work across platforms." The team has set up a channel to receive donations in Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies.

When asked to explain the several messages before the war which could be considered 'anti-regime,' he said, "We, like other citizens around the world, criticize our government and we said some points to improve the situation and deliver people's voice." He further added, "[But] we are in the middle of the war, and in the middle of the war, we sacrifice ourselves for the government who is fighting for us."

How this analysis was done

Every figure in this piece comes from one dataset: the complete public output of a single Telegram channel over 100 days, transcribed, translated, hand-coded in part and machine-coded in full. This section sets out how that dataset was built, how well the machine coding performed against my own hand coding, and which of the findings above the numbers can actually carry.

Collection

Using the Telegram API, I scraped every post published by Explosive Media (Akhbar Enfejari) between Dec. 31, 2025 and April 9, 2026 — a window chosen to bracket the 40-day U.S.–Iran war that began Feb. 28, 2026. The scrape returned 2,840 posts: 981 carrying video, 1,665 carrying images and 194 text-only dispatches. Only the channel's admin posts, so there is no comment or reply layer to account for.

To handle that volume I built a local dashboard that let me step through each post to assign categories and keywords, pull key-frame screenshots out of the videos, run optical character recognition on those frames in Persian, translate the extracted text into English, and append everything back to a master CSV. The dashboard, the scrapers and the classification scripts are in the project repository.

Transcription and translation

Video frames were extracted with FFmpeg and written to a dedicated folder under the naming convention YYYY-MM-DD-video-name-seconds.jpg, so that any frame in the analysis can be traced back to the post and the second it came from.

For on-screen text — a large share of this channel's meaning sits in Persian captions burned into the image — I used Claude Haiku 4.5 Vision, passing each frame base64-encoded and getting back the Persian text and an English translation in a single call.

Audio was transcribed with faster-whisper's medium model, running on CPU with INT8 quantization and a beam size of 3:

from faster_whisper import WhisperModel
model = WhisperModel("medium", device="cpu", compute_type="int8")
segments, info = model.transcribe(str(media_path), language="fa", beam_size=3)

Persian post captions were translated with Claude Haiku through the Anthropic API, at a total cost of roughly $0.245, into a new CSV column, message_text_english. That field — supplemented by the audio transcription where a caption was too thin to describe its own video — is the primary input every classifier below actually reads.

Both automated steps failed often enough to matter, and I found the failures while hand-coding. Transcriptions of noisy or music-heavy audio came back as garble, which sent me to the source clip to check by hand. Automatic OCR performed poorly on stylized captions; screenshotting the frame and running it through Google Lens was frequently faster and more accurate. I do not speak Farsi, but I can read the script and recognize enough vocabulary to notice when a translation had gone wrong — which is itself a limit on how far this workflow transfers to a language I could not read at all.

The hand-coded sample

To have anything to measure the machine coding against, I hand-coded a random sample of posts. The sample size comes from a calculator published on Observable by Naël Shiab, senior data producer at CBC/Radio-Canada: hand-coding 339 of the 2,840 posts yields a margin of error of about 5 percent at a 95 percent confidence level.

That interval rests on bootstrapping. Take a sample — say 18 infants' weights — and compute its mean. To bootstrap it, draw 18 weights back out of that same sample with duplicates allowed, take the mean of the draw, and repeat, thousands of times. A 95 percent confidence interval is the range covering 95 percent of those resampled means: not a statement about where the truth is, but about how much the estimate would move if the sample had come out differently.

I coded all 339 posts into 17 categories, allowing a post to carry more than one:

  • Using AI — deepfakes, tutorials for reaching American AI tools despite sanctions, deepfake analysis, or commentary on AI products.
  • Anti-regime — criticism of government policy, of Iranians' economic conditions, or demands for accountability, including posts quoting officials where the framing signals dissatisfaction with the Iranian government.
  • Pro-regime — praise of the Iranian leadership.
  • Pop culture — Iranian films, music, popular figures.
  • Foreign intervention — foreign involvement in Iranian affairs, or rejection of Reza Shah Pahlavi's leadership.
  • Iranian economy — the economy, industrial subsidies, energy conservation, economic policy, economic ministry appointments.
  • International news — world events, from Nicolás Maduro's removal from Venezuela to the Mexican cartels.
  • Religious content — references to Shia leaders such as Ali or Hussain, or images of shrines.
  • Sports — sport and sportsmen, from Messi and Ronaldo to the Iranian national teams.
  • Hamas — posts mentioning Hamas officials.
  • Gaza genocide — the genocide in Gaza, the people of Gaza, Israeli atrocities.
  • Protests — incidents that took place during the protests, reported without a value judgement.
  • Exams — exams, studying, jokes about exam nights, Iranian schools.
  • Health — the health ministry or health conditions in Iran.
  • Lego — posts showing Lego content.
  • War coverage — factual reporting on the 40-day Iran–U.S. war.
  • Weather and landscape — snowfall, rain, landscapes in Iran and elsewhere.

Eight of these carry the analysis: anti-regime, pro-regime, foreign intervention, protests, Lego, war coverage, Iranian economy and using AI. The other nine — exams, health, weather and landscape, sports, pop culture, religious content, Gaza genocide, Hamas and international news — are not the subject of this piece, but they had to be coded anyway, because without them there is no way to say what share of the channel's output the eight actually represent.

One thing about this sample needs stating plainly, because it cuts against the usual logic of a held-out benchmark. After the first classification run I reviewed the 55 posts where the model and I disagreed, and changed my own label on 31 of them — cases where a longer caption or the video itself showed I had read the post wrong. That improved the ground truth, and it also means the hand-coded set was not entirely blind to the model's output. The scores below should be read with that in mind.

Classification

Rather than one classifier deciding everything at once, I ran several in sequence. Each sees every post and answers one question, so a post can be pro-regime and war coverage and Lego without the categories competing. Every prompt takes its category definitions from the same schema I wrote for the hand coding, and the hand-coded posts were used only for evaluation, never as few-shot examples inside these prompts.

1. Stance: pro-regime vs. anti-regime. Posts went out in batches of 10 and came back as JSON, each object carrying a stance ("Pro-regime", "Anti-regime" or "Neither"), an is_primary flag recording whether the stance is the post's main subject or a secondary aspect, a confidence level and a one-sentence reason citing the wording that decided it. The prompt spelled out the boundaries that actually go wrong in this channel: that "the regime" means the Iranian state and its leadership, that criticism of the United States or Israel is not by itself anti-regime, that neutral factual reporting is Neither, that sarcasm and mockery are central to the channel's voice — a headline that ridicules the official statement beneath it is anti-regime, one that cheers it on is pro-regime — and that where a post has no text, the audio transcription is the evidence.

The first run cost about $11.54 and labelled 304 posts pro-regime, 152 anti-regime and 2,384 neither. Against the hand-coded set, Neither was solid and anti-regime was decent, but pro-regime was leaking in both directions: 27 posts I had called Neither came back pro-regime, and 15 I had called pro-regime came back Neither. Reviewing those errors changed my mind about the category rather than the prompt. Posts like a government spokesman announcing that nappies could now be bought with a commodity slip, captioned "Did a million blessings happen or did it get cheaper and we don't know?!", are not praise of the leadership, but they are not neutral either — and a definition limited to praise had nowhere to put them.

So I rewrote the schema. Pro-regime became any post that flatters the Iranian state or casts it favourably — praise of the leadership, but also official announcements relayed approvingly and framing that belittles Iran's adversaries — and the second prompt was regenerated from those revised definitions. That change is the single largest source of movement in this dataset: pro-regime went from 304 posts to 1,138, a 3.7-fold jump, while anti-regime barely moved, 152 to 156. The pro-regime numbers in this piece are therefore as much a fact about my definition as about the channel. Anti-regime turned out to be the stable category.

2. Lego. The 2,646 posts carrying a visual went to Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, judging one extracted frame each. The prompt counted minifigures and figures built from bricks, models and vehicles and buildings built from construction bricks, loose bricks and baseplates in shot, and CGI or stop-motion rendered in a brick style including photorealistic brick renders. It excluded ordinary photographs, news footage, graphics and screenshots; cartoons, 3D animation and illustration that is not brick-built; real people, vehicles and buildings; and toys that are not construction bricks. Persian text overlaid on the image was explicitly declared irrelevant — the classifier judges only what is depicted. It flagged 24 posts.

3. Five thematic categories. International news, foreign intervention, Iranian economy, war coverage and protests were run as a single multi-label pass, since 28 of the 339 hand-coded posts carry at least two of them. The prompt again took its definitions from the schema and named the boundaries that matter: international news covers world events generally, while foreign intervention is specifically foreign involvement in Iranian affairs; war coverage means the Iran–U.S. confrontation and is deliberately not restricted to posts dated after Feb. 28, because the channel was covering the coming war for weeks beforehand; protests is narrow, limited to incidents during the protests and not to be applied to general political commentary or passing mentions of unrest. Each post returned a list of zero or more categories, a single best-fitting primary, a confidence and a reason.

Accuracy, and what these numbers can carry

Each classifier's full-corpus output was compared with the 339 hand-coded posts. Precision is the share of the posts the model put in a category that belong there; recall is the share of the posts that belong there that the model found. Support is how many hand-coded posts carry the label at all — and it is the column that governs how much weight any row can bear.

Read down the precision column and the piece sorts itself into findings and gestures. Anti-regime is the one stance category that holds, at 72 percent precision and 72 percent recall, and it survived the schema rewrite almost untouched. The claim built on it needs stating precisely: the classifier found anti-regime posts throughout January and February at a rate of 5.9 percent, and after Feb. 28 it found four, 1.6 percent of post-war output — three on Feb. 28 itself and one on March 14. At the pre-war rate, 255 post-war posts would have produced about 15. That is a real collapse, not an absence, and the difference matters: because post-war posts cluster heavily on two days rather than arriving independently, the probability of seeing this few by chance alone is not negligible.

Pro-regime, in the broadened definition used throughout this piece, has a precision of 21 percent. Roughly four in five posts it labels pro-regime do not meet my hand-coded definition, almost all of them posts I coded Neither. Its recall of 97 percent says the same thing from the other side: the category is catching nearly everything, including a great deal that does not belong. Pro-regime counts and percentages in this piece should be read as indicating a direction, not as measurements — the pre- and post-war gap is large enough and the drift consistent enough to be real, but "57.3 percent pro-regime" is not a number to quote to one decimal place.

The Lego classifier scores well on a sample far too small to score: four hand-coded Lego posts, of which it found three, plus two false positives. There is also a structural reason to think it under-fires on video, since it judges a single extracted frame — 21 posts in the corpus mention animation and credit the Explosive News Team, and only nine were flagged Lego. What supports the Lego finding is not the metric but the size of the shift and the fact that all 24 flagged posts were checked by hand: five posts before the war against 19 after, eight of them on March 14 alone.

Among the thematic categories, international news and war coverage are usable. Iranian economy is marginal. Foreign intervention and protests are not usable — precision of 11 and 12 percent on supports of 10 and 3 posts. The direction reported above, that both dipped after Feb. 28, is consistent with the data but rests on categories that cannot bear it, and it should be treated as an observation awaiting better measurement rather than a finding.

Limitations

  1. The two halves of the comparison are not the same size. There are 2,585 posts across the 44 pre-war days in the window, 58.8 a day, against 255 posts across 20 post-war days, 12.8 a day. Every before-and-after percentage in this piece divides by a post-war denominator roughly a tenth the size of the pre-war one, so post-war shares move much more on a handful of posts. Weekly charts draw any week built on fewer than 20 posts hollow, on a dashed line, for this reason.
  2. The post-war side rests on two days. Of the 255 post-war posts, 108 fell on Feb. 28 and 82 on March 14 — 74.5 percent of the arm on two dates, with the remaining 65 spread across 18 days, 16 of which carry three posts or fewer. This makes the post-war figures highly sensitive to a single day: the 57.3 percent pro-regime share becomes 78.2 percent with Feb. 28 removed and 48.6 percent with March 14 removed. The direction of the change survives that test; the specific percentage does not. What the site's charts label the "week of March 9" with 82 posts is, in practice, March 14 alone.
  3. The channel went dark twice. It stopped publishing for 14 days in January and again immediately after the war began. Days with no collected posts are drawn as grey columns rather than zeroes, because a gap in collection and a gap in publishing cannot be distinguished from the outside.
  4. Per-class support is small. Three of the categories reported on rest on 18, 10, 4 and 3 hand-coded posts. At that support, a confidence interval on precision spans tens of percentage points, and one differently-coded post visibly moves the score. The 5 percent margin of error from the sample-size calculator applies to the sample as a whole, not to any single thin category within it.
  5. The pro-regime construct changed mid-project. Broadening the definition tripled the count without the underlying posts changing at all. Where this piece reports pro-regime shares, part of what is being measured is the definition.
  6. The hand-coded set was revised after seeing model disagreements — 31 labels of 55 reviewed. This makes the ground truth better and the benchmark less independent.
  7. Video is judged from a single frame for Lego, and from caption plus audio transcription for everything else. A visual that appears only outside the sampled frame is invisible to the classifier.
  8. Coding happens through translation. The classifiers read English translations of Persian captions, so translation error and classification error compound, and irony carried by Persian register or idiom is the most likely casualty in a channel whose voice is built on sarcasm.
  9. One channel, no control. There is no comparison channel here, Iranian or otherwise, so nothing separates a shift specific to Explosive Media from a shift across Persian-language Telegram during a war. The timing of the change is documented; a decision to cooperate with the Iranian government is an inference from that timing, not something these data show.
  10. Deleted posts are invisible. The scrape captures what was still standing when it ran. Anything removed beforehand — plausibly including anti-regime material, once the war started — is absent by construction, and would bias the pre-war anti-regime count downward.
  11. Large-language-model output is not deterministic. Re-running these prompts will not reproduce these labels exactly. The prediction CSVs behind every chart are published in the repository so the figures can be checked against the labels that actually produced them.
  12. These remain imperfect machine classifications. They would need several further iterations, and a larger hand-coded set for the thin categories in particular, before their precision and recall could be called credible.
