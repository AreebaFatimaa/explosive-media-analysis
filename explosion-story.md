# Meet Akhbar Enfejari, the Iranian channel behind the Lego war videos

## What three months of Telegram posts can say about the team 

By Areeba Fatima

On March 7, 2026, a new kind of war-related content hit social media platforms almost all at once. In a two-minute video, Lego Trump and Lego Netanyahu pore over an animated "Epstein File." Aggravated, Trump slams a red button, launching an airstrike on the Shajareh Tayyebeh girls' elementary school in Minab, Iran. The Lego-style animation cuts through one moment of war after another, each hard to forget: a pink schoolbag belonging to one of the girls killed in the Minab airstrike, which Amnesty International says killed 168 students.

The video shows the British base in Cyprus under attack, airstrikes in Tel Aviv, hits on the U.S. embassy in Saudi Arabia and the U.S. base in Bahrain. It shows the Strait of Hormuz shutting down, and a flag-draped coffin being carried off an aircraft by tiny Lego soldiers, and closes with a message: "In remembrance of the 168 students from Minab who were martyred at the hands of Zionist and American terrorists" — a reference to the Feb. 28 strike.

News organizations around the world rushed to react. In an on-screen analysis, a France 24 anchor [said](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-l_Iuh5Emq4), "What's incredibly interesting here is they \[the Iranian government\] are presenting to us the vulnerability of their own civilians whilst also in a way showing the United States and Israel as the aggressors."

While several journalists have taken a stab at attempting to articulate who these people really are, only few were able to speak to them, and no one has really analyzed the explosive content they were sharing on their Telegram channel, the primary source of dissemination of their message.

**The Explosive Media Telegram channel**

Explosive Media — Akhbar Enfejari in Persian — is the team of young Iranians behind the AI-generated Lego videos powering the Iranian government's war narrative on American social media. Banned from YouTube and blocked on Instagram, Facebook and X, the team now has five songs and 43,600 monthly listeners on Spotify, nearly 40,000 followers on Instagram and 79,600 on X — and has spearheaded a trend that has American media commentators declaring the White House lost the propaganda war this round.

Its longest-running accessible account in the U.S. is its Persian-language Telegram channel, set up during the 12-day war last year.

A look at what that channel was sharing in the weeks before the U.S. struck Iran in February opens a window onto Iranian society — and onto how a small group of young people came to lead the country's wartime state media efforts.

They posted weather updates, memes about exam nights, international football scores, film recommendations, foreign news and pointed criticism of the government's handling of the economy, often dozens of times a day.

Only the channel's admin posts. From Dec. 31, 2025, to April 9, 2026 — a window that brackets the 40-day U.S.-Iran war that began Feb. 28 — the channel published 2,840 messages: 981 videos, 1,665 images and 194 text dispatches.

This time frame includes 58 days before the war officially started on Feb. 28, and the 40 days of the war. 

I transcribed every post, translated it from Persian, classified it by theme and keyword, and cross-referenced by subject. Videos were sampled into key-frame screenshots. The patterns that emerge are the subject of this piece.

The channel posted several anti-regime posts before the war officially began on February 28, 2026\. The grey areas in the chart represent a lack of activity, empty blue and red boxes represent messages which did not contain media. In total, the channel has shared 1,138 posts which were classified as ‘pro-regime,’ and 156 posts classified as ‘anti-regime,’ leaving 1,546 which did not fit in either category.

A caveat on the pro-regime figures throughout this piece: measured against 339 hand-coded posts held out from the classifier, the pro-regime category returned a precision of roughly 21 percent, meaning about four in five posts it labelled pro-regime did not meet the hand-coded definition. The anti-regime and Lego classifications performed substantially better, and the major findings of this analysis rest on those two categories. Pro-regime counts and percentages should be read as indicative of direction rather than as precise measurements.

The page took a 14-day break from publishing in January, and then another right after the war began. If they had decided to collaborate with the Iranian government, it must have been after the war decided, after which they did not publish a single anti-regime post.

On February 28, the pro-regime share of the channel's output rose from 38.4% before the war to 57.3% after, while the anti-regime share fell from 5.9% to 1.6%.

Through January and February, there was a clear presence of anti-regime messaging, reaching its highest, almost 10% of all messages in the week after January 4\. After the war began, anti-regime posts entirely disappeared and the pro-regime posts were reintroduced after a week-long blackout at their highest ever point, nearing 90%. And the single well-supported post-war week of March 9, with more than 82 posts, shows a 76% jump in pro-regime messages.

While the channel appeared to be experimenting a little with LEGO-themed content, it picked up exponentially after the war. LEGO content barely existed before the war, 5 posts, 0.19% of pre-war output, and then jumps to 19 posts, 7.45% of post-war output, a roughly 39-fold increase in share. Eight of the 24 appeared on 14 March alone.

What the classification surfaced is not licensed LEGO merchandise but the channel's own brick-built productions: minifigure scenes with Persian captions, CGI renders of brick-built objects, figures holding Iranian flags, a crowd of brick-headed figures, minifigures with Molotov cocktails.

Of the 24 LEGO posts, 18 posts, 75% of all LEGO posts, were also classified pro-regime, 6 as neither, and none as anti-regime.

So LEGO animation appears to be a vehicle the channel uses for state-flattering content rather than a neutral format, and it is never used for criticism.

**The Slopaganda Duel**

The White House was already shoulder-deep in the meme world, raiding American internet culture, films and songs in a vain attempt to drum up confidence in the war — at times tipping into outright war-mongering. Officials paired strike footage released by U.S. Central Command with clips from "Iron Man," "Gladiator," "Top Gun" and the video game "Call of Duty," along with rap songs riding TikTok trends and live baseball broadcasts.

Critics swiftly labeled the output "slopaganda" — a term coined by Michał Klincewicz of Tilburg University, Australian philosopher Mark Alfano of Macquarie University and independent media researcher Amir Ebrahimi Fard.

It was lazy, in bad taste, and didn't land with most Americans, who had already turned against the war.

The Trump administration's hypermasculine, frenzied gamification of the conflict drew fire even from former White House officials. Kristopher Purcell, a Bush administration aide, [told](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/white-houses-use-of-internet-memes-to-promote-iran-war-sparks-criticism) PBS News, "This gamefication of war is really appalling, especially when you consider the administration's typical response to mass shootings, which is to blame violent video games and movies."

The Lego videos, by contrast, were a runaway hit on social media, racking up millions of views across platforms.

Calling itself the "Lego Resistance Front," the team announced in a Telegram post: "LRF has found new friends," pointing to copycat accounts that had picked up the style and produced their own Lego-style videos targeting Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, Melania Trump and other administration officials.

Writing for 404 Media, Matthew Gault put it [bluntly](https://www.404media.co/iran-is-winning-the-ai-slop-propaganda-war/): "Iran is attempting to speak to the broader American public. Trump is confident he only has to impress the online freaks he thinks still love him."

**'We love the American people'**

The BBC [interviewed](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cjd8jrd1vnyo) the team on April 11, using only a snippet in its published piece. While that segment marked the first time anyone from Explosive Media appeared on screen, face obscured in shadow, it revealed little about who they are or what they think.

"Lego is simple, Lego is charming, and Lego is understood by all," a member of the team told me when I asked why they had chosen Lego-inspired AI animation to push the Iranian government's view of the war.

In an interview, the member said the team has fewer than 10 people, all aged 25 or under. He grew up watching "Captain Tsubasa," "Brave Boy" and "Robin Hood." "We use a mix of AI tools, animation software and editing techniques, but I won't name them. That's part of the magic." The team has not been contacted by the Lego Group, he said, and relies on AI tools developed in-house or accessed via VPN to get around sanctions.

YouTube and Instagram have taken down the team's accounts repeatedly, he said. "Our pages have been shut down multiple times. But it's never really stopped us. We have friends all over the world who keep sharing our work across platforms." The team has set up a channel to receive donations in Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies. 

When asked to explain the several messages before the war which could be considered ‘anti-regime,’ he said, “We, like other citizens around the world, criticize our government and we said some points to improve the situation and deliver people’s voice.” He further added, “\[But\] we are in the middle of the war, and in the middle of the war, we sacrifice ourselves for the government who is fighting for us.
